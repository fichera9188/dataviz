01 / THE WORLD

First, put the average
in perspective.

How much a country drinks is one question. How its people drink is another. Start with the global picture, then look beyond the average.

02 / CHANGING HABITS

The long view is
far from a straight line.

Alcohol availability peaked in the 1970s, rose again in the early 2020s, and has since fallen. The drinks making up that total have changed too.

Among 18–24-year-olds, monthly high-volume drinking fell from 57.3% to 40.8%. Among people aged 60–69, it rose from 12.8% to 19.4%.

03 / AGE & GENDER

Different people.
Different kinds of risk.

Men and women report different drinking patterns, and weekly volume is only part of the picture. Occasional high-volume drinking can occur even below the weekly threshold.

READ THE MEASURES

A percentage describes how common a behaviour is. A volume describes how much alcohol is available. A count describes events. Each answers a different question.

04 / DIFFERENT PLACES

Where you look
changes what you see.

Prevalence and treatment demand are two different views of geography. Equal-size state tiles compare proportions; circles on the map compare the number of recorded treatment episodes.

In 2022–23, risky-drinking prevalence ranged from 26.4% in Victoria to 40.4% in the Northern Territory. That is a different comparison from the size of the treatment caseload.

05 / LASTING CONSEQUENCES

A falling average
doesn’t erase the burden.

Changes in supply and reported habits do not immediately translate into fewer hospitalisations or deaths. Consequences have their own age patterns, timescales and definitions.

THE PATTERN THAT MATTERS

There is no single drinking story.
The average changes. The differences remain.

Understanding alcohol means looking at who drinks, how they drink, and which consequences follow—with a clear measure for each question.

About this story: purpose, data and design

Why & who

This story helps general readers understand how alcohol patterns vary across age, gender and geography. Global estimates provide context for the detailed Australian case study.

What

Published WHO, AIHW, ABS and ASSAD aggregate data. Survey percentages, supply, treatment episodes, hospitalisations and mortality remain distinct measures. The data snapshot was collected on 13 October 2026.

How

Maps show geographic patterns; lines show time; heatmaps compare ages across waves; connected dots show gender gaps. Mosaic areas show overlapping thresholds; grouped hospital bars and ranked burden bars support count comparisons; the mortality waterfall explains accumulated changes. All maps and charts render through Vega-Lite.

Colour meanings

Teal is reserved for males and plum for females. Single series use charcoal. Light-to-dark ochre shows increasing magnitude; brick and olive distinguish mortality increases and decreases. A population filter changes the selected data, not what a colour means.

Why these chart choices?

Each chart answers a different question. Expand an explanation to see its purpose, the alternative considered and its limits.

01 · World consumption

Question. Where do estimated consumption levels differ geographically?

Why this form. A choropleth places consumption estimates in their geographic context, making broad spatial patterns visible.

Alternative. Ranked bars would compare country values more precisely but would hide their geographic arrangement.

Limit. Large countries attract more visual attention. Grey means no matched estimate, not zero consumption.

02 · Regional drinking distributions

Question. How variable are country estimates within each region, and where does Australia sit?

Why this form. Boxplots summarise the median, middle half and full range of many country estimates without a crowded list.

Alternative. Regional average bars would conceal how different the countries within a region can be.

Limit. Each country has equal weight. Whiskers show the minimum and maximum, not the usual 1.5-IQR rule; country counts and quartiles are in the tooltip.

03 · Six decades of availability

Question. How did Australian alcohol availability change over time?

Why this form. Position on one time axis supports reading a continuous trajectory across six decades.

Alternative. Annual bars would emphasise individual years and make the overall path harder to follow.

Limit. Availability measures supply per person aged 15+, not an individual's reported intake.

04 · Beverage composition

Question. How has the composition of available pure alcohol changed?

Why this form. A 100% stacked area chart communicates composition and change together. The main latest-year shares are directly labelled.

Alternative. Separate lines would make individual trajectories easier to compare but weaken the immediate part-to-whole reading.

Limit. Middle bands do not have a common baseline. Hover for exact shares, including cider's small share; these are pure-alcohol shares, not beverage-volume shares.

05 · Age and survey waves

Question. Which age groups report higher percentages, and how does the pattern differ across survey waves?

Why this form. A heatmap fits many age-by-wave combinations into a readable matrix with chronological columns and ordered age groups.

Alternative. Multiple lines would help trace one group precisely but become crowded when all groups are shown.

Limit. The 0â€“60% scale stays fixed across measures. Blank cells are missing or unsuitable estimates, and each wave samples different people.

06 · Adolescent drinking

Question. What does the selected reported percentage look like out of 100 percentage points?

Why this form. A waffle supports a focused, accessible part-to-whole explanation of one selected group and drinking period.

Alternative. A bar would be more compact and better for simultaneous comparison of many categories.

Limit. Squares are percentage points, not individual students. A fractional square preserves the published decimal estimate.

07 · Adult gender gap

Question. How large is the maleâ€“female difference within each adult age group?

Why this form. Two connected positions on the same percentage axis make within-age differences visible with little visual clutter.

Alternative. Grouped bars would work, but the distance between estimates is less explicit.

Limit. These are source-specific survey estimates. Differences describe association, not a causal explanation.

08 · Overlapping thresholds

Question. How do weekly volume and monthly high-volume occasions overlap?

Why this form. A mosaic represents the joint structure of two thresholds as well as the five category shares. Abstainers are separate.

Alternative. A pie or a single stacked bar would show category shares without making the two-threshold organisation as explicit.

Limit. Cell heights are conditional within columns. Weekly-only and episodic-only are different patterns, not an ordered severity scale. Areas are normalised only for published rounding.

09 · State prevalence

Question. How does risky-drinking prevalence differ among states and territories?

Why this form. A tile cartogram retains approximate geography while preventing large land areas from dominating the comparison.

Alternative. A conventional Australian choropleth would give large states much more visual weight.

Limit. Tile positions are approximate. The fixed 0â€“60% scale encodes the selected survey population's percentage; counts and land area are not encoded.

10 · Recorded treatment demand

Question. Where are recorded alcohol treatment episodes concentrated geographically?

Why this form. A proportional-symbol map combines location with the size of recorded caseload, contrasting with prevalence in the neighbouring chart.

Alternative. Sorted bars would rank counts more precisely; exact labels and the size legend support comparison here.

Limit. Episodes are not unique clients or population-adjusted rates, and do not establish unmet demand. ACT's displaced circle is connected to its location.

11 · Hospitalisations by age and sex

Question. How do recorded hospitalisation counts differ by age group and sex?

Why this form. Grouped horizontal bars make positive counts directly comparable within age groups and leave room for exact count labels.

Alternative. A mirrored pyramid introduces negative plotting positions and resembles a population structure despite uneven age bands.

Limit. Counts describe episodes, not unique people or age-specific risk. The open-ended 55+ group includes more ages; the national total includes categories outside these bars.

12 · Disease burden contributions

Question. Which listed disease groups contribute most to alcohol-attributable healthy life years lost?

Why this form. Six groups fit on a shared zero baseline with readable names, counts and tooltip shares. Wider views also label the shares directly.

Alternative. A treemap provides a composition overview, but comparing areas and fitting labels is less effective for this ranking question.

Limit. The residual is the total minus the five listed groups, not an invented disease category. The same count scale is used for every selected sex; colour identifies listed values versus the residual.

13 · Mortality changes over time

Question. How did yearly increases and decreases add up to the difference between 2015 and 2024?

Why this form. A waterfall makes the arithmetic contribution of each year's change explicit while retaining the starting and ending totals.

Alternative. A line would show the trajectory, but would not separate the contributions to the final difference as directly.

Limit. Increases and decreases explain the arithmetic, not its causes. Recent counts can be revised; alcohol-induced deaths are narrower than all alcohol-attributable deaths.

Source tables are converted to compact chart data without replacing missing values with zeros. Mosaic proportions are normalised for published rounding only; the burden residual and mortality differences are documented derivations.
