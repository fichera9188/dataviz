01 / THE WORLD

First, put the average
in perspective.

How much a country drinks is one question. How its people drink is another. Start with the global picture, then look beyond the average.

02 / CHANGING HABITS

The long view is
far from a straight line.

Alcohol availability peaked in the 1970s, rose again in the early 2020s, and has since fallen. The drinks making up that total have changed too.

Among 18–24-year-olds, monthly high-volume drinking fell from 57.3% to 40.8%. Among people aged 60–69, it rose from 12.8% to 19.4%.

03 / AGE & GENDER

Different people.
Different kinds of risk.

Men and women report different drinking patterns, and weekly volume is only part of the picture. Occasional high-volume drinking can occur even below the weekly threshold.

READ THE MEASURES

A percentage describes how common a behaviour is. A volume describes how much alcohol is available. A count describes events. Each answers a different question.

04 / DIFFERENT PLACES

Where you look
changes what you see.

Prevalence and treatment demand are two different views of geography. Equal-size state tiles compare proportions; circles on the map compare the number of recorded treatment episodes.

In 2022–23, risky-drinking prevalence ranged from 26.4% in Victoria to 40.4% in the Northern Territory. That is a different comparison from the size of the treatment caseload.

05 / LASTING CONSEQUENCES

A falling average
doesn’t erase the burden.

Changes in supply and reported habits do not immediately translate into fewer hospitalisations or deaths. Consequences have their own age patterns, timescales and definitions.

THE PATTERN THAT MATTERS

There is no single drinking story.
The average changes. The differences remain.

Understanding alcohol means looking at who drinks, how they drink, and which consequences follow—with a clear measure for each question.

About this story: purpose, data and design

Why & who

This story helps general readers understand how alcohol patterns vary across age, gender and geography. Global estimates provide context for the detailed Australian case study.

What

Published WHO, AIHW, ABS and ASSAD aggregate data. Survey percentages, supply, treatment episodes, hospitalisations and mortality remain distinct measures. The data snapshot was collected on 13 October 2026.

How

Maps show geographic patterns; lines show time; heatmaps compare ages across waves; connected dots show gender gaps. Mosaic areas show joint risk categories, while burden and mortality charts describe consequences. All maps and charts render through Vega-Lite.

Source tables are converted to compact chart data without replacing missing values with zeros. Mosaic proportions are normalised for published rounding only; treemap coordinates and mortality differences are documented derivations. Machine-readable sources · All chart specifications
